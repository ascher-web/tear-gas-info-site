When the smoke clears, what happens to the human body?

Tear gas is known for burning eyes, coughing, and trouble breathing. But medical research shows its effects can go further, including lung complications, eye and skin injuries, and reported changes in reproductive health.

Why this matters

Tear gas doesn't only reach the people it's aimed at. Once it's in the air, it can expose bystanders, residents, workers, and people with existing health conditions. How badly someone is affected depends on the chemical, how much there is, how long they're exposed, whether it's indoors or outdoors, and their own health.

Many people recover soon after leaving the area. Heavier or repeated exposure, though, has been linked to longer-lasting symptoms, and serious and permanent injuries have been documented. There are still big unanswered questions about repeated exposure, pregnancy, fertility, and long-term health.

How to read the evidence

Each section on this site is labeled so you know how strong the science is:

  • Established effect Supported by consistent clinical evidence (like eye irritation and coughing).
  • Observed association Studies found a link, but it isn't proven to be the cause (like menstrual changes).
  • Insufficient evidence Not enough research to say (like effects on fertility or miscarriage).
